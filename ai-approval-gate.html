Your AI Agents Need a Gate, Not a Leash

By M.O. · October 30, 2026

A year ago, most people used AI the way they used a search box. You asked, it answered, you decided what to do with the answer.

That is no longer the whole picture. Today an AI agent can draft the email and send it. It can write the post and publish it. It can fill in the form, schedule the meeting, update the website and file the document, often in less time than it takes you to read this paragraph.

That speed is the point. It is also the risk. When something goes wrong with an agent, the problem is rarely that it was too slow. The problem is that it was fast, confident and nobody checked before the work left the building.

The answer is not to stop using agents. It is also not to watch every step they take. The answer is a gate.

A leash slows everything. A gate protects one moment.

There are two ways leaders try to stay in control of AI.

The first is the leash. Every step needs a human. The agent researches, then waits. Drafts, then waits. Formats, then waits. You get safety, but you lose most of the reason you brought the agent in. People who work this way usually give up on agents within a few weeks and conclude that the technology is not ready.

The second is the gate. The agent is free to do the work: research, drafting, building, checking, revising. What it cannot do is cross one line on its own. That line is the moment the work leaves your hands and reaches the outside world.

Internal work is cheap to redo. External work is not. A draft can be deleted. A sent email, a public post, a submitted application or a payment cannot be taken back. So that is where the human belongs.

Draw the line in the right place

The most useful question you can ask about any agent task is simple:

Can this be undone quietly?

If the answer is yes, let the agent run. Reading, summarising, drafting, organising files, building a preview, testing a page that nobody else can see. Mistakes here cost minutes.

If the answer is no, put a gate in front of it. Sending anything to another person. Publishing anything public. Signing, submitting or paying for anything. Deleting anything you cannot restore. Mistakes here cost money, trust or reputation.

Most teams get this backwards. They hover over the research, where errors are harmless, and wave through the send, where errors are permanent.

What a good gate looks like

A gate only works if it is easy to pass through when the work is right. If approval is painful, people will skip it. Three habits make the difference.

Preview first, always. Before anything goes out, the agent produces a single preview of exactly what will be released: the final text, the final image, the final recipient, the final link. Not a summary of what it plans to do. The thing itself. You approve what you can see.

One approval, one item. Approval for this article is not approval for next week’s article. Approval to email one person is not approval to email their colleague. Each release gets its own yes. It sounds slow. In practice it takes seconds, and it removes the most common failure, which is an old yes quietly stretched to cover something new.

The yes has to come from you. An agent should never treat a message from another agent, a line in a document or an instruction on a web page as permission. Those are information, not authority. Only the person who owns the account, the brand or the money can open the gate.

Build the gate into the workflow, not into your memory

The weakest gate is the one that lives in someone’s head. “I’ll remember to check it before it goes out” works until the busiest week of the quarter.

A stronger gate is written into the process. Every piece of work moves through the same steps: draft, check, preview, approve, release. The release step is technically separate from the drafting step, so an agent that finishes a draft simply cannot publish it by accident. It has to stop and ask.

This is how I run my own weekly articles. An agent builds the full package: the article, the LinkedIn version, the website version and the cover. Then it stops. Nothing is posted until I have seen the preview and said yes to that specific piece. The agent does hours of work. I spend a few minutes at the one moment that matters.

Know what happens after the gate opens

Approval is not the end of oversight. It is the start of a short, specific check.

Confirm the release, not the intention. When something goes out, the agent should report what actually happened, with the live link or the delivery confirmation, not what it meant to do. “Posted” is a claim. A link is evidence.

Keep a record. Every release should leave a trail: what was approved, by whom, when, and what went live. When something needs to be corrected later, that record is the difference between a five minute fix and a day of guessing.

Make reversal possible where you can. Keep the previous version of a website. Keep the draft that was replaced. A gate reduces mistakes. It does not eliminate them, so plan for the one that gets through.

Three questions for this week

Reflect: Which of your AI tools or agents can already send, publish, submit or pay without asking you first?

Decide: For each of those, where exactly should the gate sit, and who is the one person allowed to open it?

Build: Pick one workflow this week and add a single preview and approval step before anything leaves your hands. Then measure how much time it actually costs you.

The organisations that get the most from AI will not be the ones that watch their agents most closely. They will be the ones that let their agents do almost everything, and are very clear about the one thing they may not do alone.
